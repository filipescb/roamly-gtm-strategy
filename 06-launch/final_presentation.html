01 · GTM STRATEGY

The problem, the audience, the motion

The problem worth solving

Group travelers already want to book, coordinate, and pay for trips on Roamly — but the product only supports individual bookings. This forces groups into manual workarounds (separate accounts, side payment apps, DIY availability syncing), driving high drop-off and generating Support tickets. Demand has outgrown what the product supports.

Target segment

The trip organizer coordinating a multi-day, multi-activity celebration trip for 6+ people (bachelorette, reunion, milestone birthday) — full buying authority, but chasing friends for payments, juggling fragmented bookings, and tracking who's confirmed.

Chosen GTM motion

PLG · primaryCLG · secondary

Minutes to value, buyer and user are the same person, self-serve, and it scales via product virality — every group trip pulls new, unacquired people in. CLG amplifies this through Roamly's existing travel-community distribution. No SLG in v1 — a deliberate scope cut for a segment (corporate offsites) not being targeted yet.

02 · COMPETITIVE INTELLIGENCE & STRATEGIC BET

Battlecard highlights & the bet

Where the bet actually pays off

Illustrative model, not measured data — the shape of the argument from the M2 battlecard: below the complexity threshold, the free DIY stack wins on effort alone.

Status quo (DIY): strengths

  • Zero adoption friction — no download, no account
  • Totally flexible, free, already running

Status quo (DIY): weaknesses

  • All coordination labor sits on one person
  • No visibility, no enforcement, breaks down at scale

Trap to set

"What happens the next time someone in the group 'forgets' to Venmo you back — and how would you even know, three weeks later, who still owes what?"

Objection → Response

"It's free, this isn't."Reframe cost as time and social friction, not money — the organizer already pays with hours of chasing.
"My friends won't sign up."Guests engage as guests — pay and view the itinerary without creating an account.

The strategic bet

We are betting that Roamly Groups wins by becoming the system of record for complex group trips, because split payments is already commoditized (Airbnb, free, since 2018) — the real, unowned gap is the connective tissue between the payment, the itinerary, and the activity.

05 · PRICING RECOMMENDATION

Model, packaging, and what to validate

Model

Transaction-based

Existing per-booking commission, applied across more participants. No new, visible fee to the organizer.

Who pays

Each traveler pays for their own experience booking, as today. The organizer pays nothing extra for coordination.

Packaging

No tiers

One audience, one included capability — no features/accounts/usage tiering. Tiering would reintroduce the friction the product removes.

Research to validate

Not Van Westendorp / Max-Diff / Conjoint — no fee is being priced. Instead: a live usage pilot with existing repeat bookers.

Why the model works: revenue per trip

Illustrative, not actual figures — commission scales with participants per trip, not a new fee, so revenue grows directly with group size.

Validate first

That group trips generate meaningfully more revenue per trip than an equivalent solo booking — measured via booking completion rate, revenue per trip, and guest-to-signup conversion. If this doesn't hold, there is no monetization mechanism left, since the organizer is charged nothing directly.

The aha moment

The battlecard's "Why You Lose" analysis showed the original segment was defined on the wrong axis — by trip occasion (bachelorette, reunion) rather than trip complexity (group size, number of bookings). A 4-person trip and a 12-person trip behave completely differently against the free DIY status quo, and no amount of positioning fixes that gap below a certain complexity threshold.

Key takeaways

  • Narrow the segment by coordination complexity, not just occasion
  • Win on the connective layer (booking + payment + itinerary), not on a commoditized mechanic
  • Price to remove objections, not to maximize day-one revenue
  • One well-justified audience beats three vague ones
